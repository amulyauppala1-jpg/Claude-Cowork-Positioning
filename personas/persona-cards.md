# Personas

**Note for Claude Design:** each H1 below is one artboard. Eight total, landscape 1280×720. Six are persona cards sharing an identical layout. Pre-read and Proof points are reference pages and should look deliberately different from the cards.

Each persona card has four tiers, largest to smallest:

1. **ON THE CARD** — the meeting. *Lead with* is the hero, in the largest type on the page. *Don't open with* stacks above *Don't say* in a left column; *They'll ask* fills a right column of equal weight. *Who they are* sits above the hero in small type.
2. **POSITIONING** — full width, above a hairline, at body size. Marketing and product read this, so it must not look like a footnote.
3. **REASONING** — three columns, one step smaller than body.
4. **NOTES** — smallest readable type, at the bottom, under a rule.

Two families only, and the serif does exactly two jobs: the persona name and the *Lead with* line. Everything below the hero is one sans at three sizes, one per tier.

The two-column tables are headerless: phrase on the left, reason on the right.

Nothing may clip. The densest cards are CHRO, which carries an extra **Demo this** block, and General Counsel buying for legal, which carries four reasoning blocks and no notes. Check those two first. If something doesn't fit, change the layout or the type scale, not the copy.

Use only this copy. Don't rewrite, shorten, improve or add to it. Every line is deliberate.

# Pre-read

The same product with the same three value props still needs a different story for each buyer.

**The three value props.**

| Value prop | In plain terms |
| :--- | :--- |
| Covers every team | One deployment across the company on one contract, instead of a separate AI tool per team |
| Work comes back finished | Finishes multi-step tasks and hands the team completed output, instead of helping someone write a first draft |
| Set up for how your team works | Plugins configure it for a function, wired into the tools that team already uses |

These are internal shorthand. They are not opening lines. The sentence you actually say is on each card, under **Lead with**.

**Assumptions.** These shaped every choice in the cards that follow.

**Every buyer here is an executive purchasing for a department, not for themselves.** A CHRO isn't making the policy updates and a CRO isn't the rep who is logging in to prep for their upcoming calls.

Every card is written in two steps to reflect the outcomes they are looking for their departments to achieve with an agentic workspace.

**The General Counsel is two roles.** They buy for legal, and they approve every other function's purchase. These are different use cases, reflected in the persona cards.

**All buyer research is public.** Surveys, trade press and published interviews, cited on each card. None of it came from call recordings, win/loss notes or a customer quote database, which is where this would really come from.

# CFO

**ON THE CARD**

**Who they are:** Holds the budget for a company-wide rollout. Judged on predictable costs and accurate forecasts, so a surprise is worse than a big number.

**Lead with:** "Your AI spend becomes one predictable line instead of something that grows every time a team buys a tool."

**Don't open with:** "One deployment, one contract, one security review." That describes the product. It doesn't tell a CFO what changes for them.

**Don't say:**

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Productivity | Burned word. Their last AI project was sold on it and didn't produce a number |
| Efficiency, with no number | Reads as a dodge |
| Headcount savings | They'll hold you to it at renewal |

**They'll ask:**

"We already pay for Copilot and ChatGPT. Why a third line item?"

"Show me the ROI. Our last AI project never produced a number."

"What happens to this cost when usage grows?"

**POSITIONING**

**Positioning:** *For a CFO whose company is already paying for AI in three or more places, who needs the AI line to become predictable instead of growing team by team, Cowork is a single agentic workspace deployed once across every function that replaces per-team subscriptions with one vendor, one contract and one security review. Unlike buying a separate AI tool for each function, Cowork provides one deployment covering everyone, which makes this a consolidation rather than an addition.*

**REASONING**

**Why it ranks first:** Their company already pays for AI in three or four places, so the useful part is that this replaces those instead of joining the queue. Worker access to AI rose 50% in 2025, and only one in five companies has mature governance for autonomous AI agents ([Deloitte, State of AI in the Enterprise 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/what-we-do/capabilities/applied-artificial-intelligence/content/state-of-ai-in-the-enterprise.html), 3,235 senior leaders across 24 countries). Sprawl is arriving faster than anyone is controlling it, and it arrives as a budget line. Not leading with "does the work" is deliberate, because that is what every AI vendor has told this person for three years.

**NOTES**

**Note:** "What happens when usage grows" is the sharpest question they ask. Agents consume more than chat, so it goes straight at forecast accuracy. And if a function head is the one buying, the CFO drops to approver and the whole ranking flips.

# CMO

**ON THE CARD**

**Who they are:** Owns the marketing budget. Leadership now expects everything the department does to be quantifiable, and the AI spend is the part they can't yet account for.

**Lead with:** "Work comes back finished, not as drafts your team still has to review."

**Don't open with:** "Agents complete campaign work end to end." That is the same fact stated as a capability, and this buyer has heard capability claims before.

**Don't say:**

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Content at scale, 10x your content | The worst thing you could say. That's their problem, not their goal |
| Productivity | Implies commodity output |
| Replace your agency | A procurement fight they didn't ask for |

**They'll ask:**

"We already spend 15% of budget on AI. What did that buy me?"

"How do I stop this producing off-brand content at scale?"

"My team isn't ready for this."

**POSITIONING**

**Positioning:** *For a marketing leader who has already committed budget to AI and can't show what it returned, who needs finished work rather than more drafts to review, Cowork is an agentic workspace configured for marketing that completes campaign work end to end, with brand review built into the workflow. Unlike chat assistants and content tools that produce drafts someone still has to finish, Cowork provides completed work and a review step, so the time saved doesn't go straight back into reviewing.*

**REASONING**

**Why it ranks first:** The share of marketing leaders whose leadership expects fully quantifiable results for everything the department does rose from 59% to 69% between 2023 and 2025 ([CMSWire, State of the CMO 2025](https://www.cmswire.com/digital-marketing/why-the-cmo-job-is-being-rewritten-in-real-time-and-how-to-land-on-the-right-side/), 500+ marketing leaders). Meanwhile around 15% of the average marketing budget already goes to AI ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities)). So the sale isn't "try AI." What they bought made more drafts to review.

**Make it concrete:** "Finished work" means the weekly performance report arriving already built, with numbers pulled and insights drafted. Anthropic's own marketing team took that from a day or two down to about two hours ([Marketing ops at Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/how-anthropics-marketing-operations-team-uses-claude-cowork-to-automate-reporting-and-campaign-builds)). That is a result for a board meeting. "Our team writes faster" isn't.

**NOTES**

**Note:** "Off-brand content at scale" is a procurement checklist item now rather than a personal worry, so a vague answer fails in writing. In a company-wide rollout this person becomes your champion, and champions need something forwardable to the CFO, written in finance's language.

# CRO

**ON THE CARD**

**Who they are:** Owns revenue. Judged on quota attainment, win rate, and how long deals take.

**Lead with:** "Your forecast and pipeline review, built from what the team already has, with no CRM cleanup first."

**Don't open with:** "Configured to your sales motion." That is a product setting. Lead with what it produces, which is the forecast, without the cleanup first.

**Don't say:**

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Another platform, another dashboard | Their reps already feel buried in tools |
| AI SDR | Crowded category with a bad reputation |
| Replace reps | They need those reps to hit a number this quarter |

**They'll ask:**

"My reps won't use it."

"Our CRM data is a mess. What's it even working from?"

"We cancelled our last AI project."

**POSITIONING**

**Positioning:** *For a revenue leader whose reps ignore the sales AI they already own, who needs tooling shaped around how their team already sells, Cowork is an agentic workspace configured to the sales motion that produces forecasts, pipeline reviews and call prep from what the team already has, even when the CRM isn't connected. Unlike sales AI whose payoff depends on reps adopting it first, Cowork puts the first output in the leader's own hands.*

**REASONING**

**Why it ranks first:** About one in five reps uses the AI features already in their tools ([Salesforce, State of Sales 2026](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/state-of-sales-report-announcement-2026/)). That makes adoption the risk to answer, not capability. So lead with the forecast and pipeline review, which land in the CRO's own hands on day one without waiting on rep adoption.

**NOTES**

**Note:** The CRM objection is answerable, because the plugin also runs on call notes, transcripts and spreadsheets. On "we cancelled our last AI project," ask what and why before you answer, since a cost overrun and a useless tool are different problems. And concede the honest part: automating a step often exposes the data problem underneath instead of removing it. A sitting CRO said exactly that in public, so pretending otherwise costs you credibility.

# CHRO

**ON THE CARD**

**Who they are:** Owns people, and increasingly owns AI governance for the whole company.

**Lead with:** "Your team gets more done, and nothing new lands on your risk register."

**Don't open with:** "Operates against your written policy with session-level auditability." That is how it works. Lead with what it keeps off their risk register.

**Demo this:** Onboarding and policy work, not performance reviews or comp. Those two are the plugin's headline features and also the two most legally exposed areas in HR. The impressive features trigger the objection before you've earned the right to answer it. This is the opposite of the advice for every other buyer.

**Don't say:**

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Headcount, FTE savings, do more with less | Boards are already pressing CHROs on headcount. This puts you inside that conversation |
| Automate HR | Documented human judgment is the thing protecting them |
| Screen candidates automatically | Straight into the bias precedent everyone in HR knows |

**They'll ask:**

"If it produces a biased outcome, who's liable?"

"Does this create a new high-risk system under the EU AI Act?"

"Is this going to be used to justify cuts?"

**POSITIONING**

**Positioning:** *For a people leader accountable for AI outcomes their employer can be sued over, who needs AI that works from their own written policy and leaves a record, Cowork is an agentic workspace configured to the organization's HR policy that handles onboarding, policy and documentation work with session-level auditability. Unlike general-purpose AI pointed at HR work with no policy behind it and no audit trail, Cowork provides work that traces back to the company's own policy and holds up in a review.*

**REASONING**

**Why it ranks first:** This is the same value prop the CRO leads with, for the opposite reason. For sales, configuration solves adoption. For HR, it solves liability. If an AI tool produces a biased outcome in hiring, performance or pay, the employer is liable, not the vendor ([IMD](https://www.imd.org/ibyimd/human-resources/ai-and-the-chro-redefining-human-capital-leadership/)). The capacity lands on their team. The liability lands on them.

**NOTES**

**Note:** On liability, say plainly that it sits with the employer. That is exactly why configuration and an audit trail matter, and a reassuring non-answer fails here. Route the AI Act question to legal instead of guessing.

"Is this for cuts" usually goes unasked and matters more than most of what does get said. You answer it by choosing which use cases you demo.

Two things to check before the meeting. The EU AI Act deadline fell in August 2026, so confirm that is still recent enough to lean on. And works councils can block deployment outright in Europe.

# General Counsel, buying for legal

**ON THE CARD**

**Who they are:** The economic buyer for their own team, and a more enthusiastic one than most people expect.

**Lead with:** "NDA and contract queues triaged before a lawyer opens them."

**Don't open with:** "Clause-by-clause review against your playbook." That is how it works. What they care about is the queue getting shorter.

**Don't say:**

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Replace lawyers | Nothing here replaces judgment |
| Automated legal advice | Edges into unauthorized practice of law, which is a category error rather than just bad taste |
| Anything softening attorney review | The product's own docs say it. Stating a limit earns more trust than claiming it away |

**They'll ask:**

"How much work is it to get our playbook in?"

"How does this compare to purpose-built legal AI?"

"What's the privilege position on work done here?"

**POSITIONING**

**Positioning:** *For an in-house legal team reviewing more contracts than it can staff, who needs review against their own risk posture rather than a generic standard, Cowork is an agentic workspace configured with the team's contract playbook that triages and reviews clause by clause against it, with attorney review kept in place. Unlike generic AI review applying a standard it inferred rather than the one you set, Cowork provides analysis against your playbook, with the human review line stated rather than glossed over.*

**REASONING**

**Why it ranks first:** More literally here than anywhere else, review runs against their own playbook. Without it you get generic output. For legal, configuration is the product.

**On setup:** Every plugin opens with a short interview that asks how the team actually works, covering the playbook, who things escalate to, where they draw the risk line, and the house style. Twelve pre-built practice-area plugins mean nobody starts from an empty file, and teams add their own precedents from there ([Claude for the legal industry](https://claude.com/blog/claude-for-the-legal-industry)). Don't sell it as zero effort. The setup is the work, and saying that out loud is what makes the rest of the pitch credible.

**On the comparison:** Harvey, Ironclad, iManage, NetDocuments, Docusign and more than twenty others ship as MCP connectors. So the honest answer to "how does this compare to Harvey" is that it doesn't compete, because Harvey plugs in. Cowork sits over the legal tools a firm already owns, which is why the playbook applies across all of them instead of inside one product. Say that, and the comparison stops being a fight you have to win.

**The assumption most people get wrong:** Legal is not the reluctant function. After the legal plugin launched it became the number-one power-user function in Cowork, at over three times the usage of any other ([Anthropic's Associate General Counsel](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2026/05/12/al-interview-mark-pike-anthropic-associate-general-counsel/)). That proves it can happen. It tells you nothing about the GC in your meeting. Walking in with "legal is usually our biggest adopter" and meeting a cautious GC reads as pressure, and caution is the correct posture for that job.

# General Counsel, reviewing someone else's purchase

**ON THE CARD**

**You are not the one pitching here.** Your champion is, in a meeting you're not in. No value prop leads, because the GC isn't evaluating whether the product is good. They're looking for a reason to stop, delay or condition it. An asset that opens with a benefit statement reads as someone who doesn't understand what the meeting is for.

**This is the more common encounter.** A GC touches every other function's deal and only occasionally buys their own.

**What they'll ask, in roughly this order, and what answers it:**

| They ask | What answers it |
| :--- | :--- |
| Where is data processed and retained? | The Compliance API, which covers Cowork sessions across desktop, web and mobile |
| Does content train a model? | No position on this yet. Escalate, don't improvise |
| What does the audit trail look like? | Session-level records through the same Compliance API |
| Do outputs touch regulated decisions? | Depends on the function. HR is the exposed one |
| Who's liable when it's wrong? | The customer. Say so plainly, because a reassuring non-answer fails here |

**REASONING**

**Arm the champion with these answers in advance.** A champion ambushed in a review meeting loses the deal at a meeting you're not in.

**NOTES**

**Note:** Who sits in the room is reasoned from the product's stated buyer, not from deals anyone actually ran.
